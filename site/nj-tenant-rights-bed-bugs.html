Bed Bugs and Tenant Rights in New Jersey

New Jersey2 min read

What New Jersey tenants and landlords should know about bed bugs, from the warranty of habitability to access notice and who pays when an infestation spreads.

Bed bug disputes in New Jersey usually come down to two questions. Who has to fix it, and who has to pay? The answers aren't always simple, but the basic framework is clearer than most people think.

This is general information, not legal advice. Tenants can contact their local housing office or a legal aid organization, and landlords should check with counsel.

A right to a livable home

New Jersey has recognized an implied warranty of habitability in residential leases since the state Supreme Court's 1970 decision in Marini v. Ireland. In short, a landlord promises that the apartment is fit to live in, and a serious condition the landlord doesn't fix can give the tenant legal remedies. A significant pest infestation is the kind of condition tenants often raise.

When it's the owner's job

The state's maintenance code for multiple dwellings, N.J.A.C. 5:10-10.2, makes owners responsible for eradicating insects when an infestation is in two or more units or in common areas. In an apartment building in Elizabeth or New Brunswick, bed bugs rarely stay in one unit for long, so this rule ends up covering most real-world cases.

When the tenant shares responsibility

The code also says occupants are responsible for violations they had the power to prevent, such as those caused by their willful act or gross negligence. A tenant who refuses treatment, or keeps dragging infested furniture back in from the curb, may end up sharing the blame.

Access and notice

Tenants have to let owners in for inspections and repairs. Under N.J.A.C. 5:10-5.1, one day's notice is generally reasonable in a multiple dwelling, and immediate access is allowed in real emergencies. Most bed bug inspections can be scheduled with notice. A tenant who won't open the door makes everyone's problem worse, including their neighbors'.

Why the inspection method matters to both sides

For tenants, a reliable inspection means the problem gets found and treated, not argued about. For landlords, it means treatment decisions are based on something solid. A detection dog that alerts falsely can lead to treatments and disputes nobody needed. One that misses bed bugs lets an infestation spread to the units next door.

Ask whoever's doing the inspection for their Tri-State K9 team number, and look it up. Certified teams have passed a blind field test on live bed bugs within the past year. That's a fair starting point for both sides of the lease.

Sources

  • N.J.A.C. 5:10-10.2 (insects, rats and vermin)
  • N.J.A.C. 5:10-5.1 (responsibility of occupants)
