NYC Bed Bug Disclosure Laws and Tenant Rights: A Plain-English Overview

Law and compliance2 min read

An overview of New York City bed bug disclosure rules, annual reporting and tenant rights, and why accurate K9 inspection records matter for landlords.

New York takes bed bugs seriously on paper, and landlords who don't keep up with the rules tend to find out the hard way. This is a general overview, not legal advice, and the rules do get updated, so check current requirements with HPD or your attorney before you rely on any of it.

Disclosure before a lease is signed

Under New York State law, owners renting apartments in New York City have to tell new tenants about bed bug history for the past year, covering both the apartment and the building. It's done on a standard Bedbug Disclosure Form from NYS Homes and Community Renewal, and tenants are entitled to see it before they sign.

The idea is simple. A renter moving into a building in Sunset Park or Inwood ought to know whether the place has had a problem recently. A landlord who hides it is setting up a dispute later.

Annual reporting to the city

Under Local Law 69 of 2017, owners of multiple dwellings also have to file an annual bed bug report with HPD between December 1 and December 31. It covers November 1 through October 31 of the prior year: the number of units, how many had infestations, how many were treated, and how many had bed bugs again after treatment. Owners then have to give tenants a copy at each new lease or renewal, or post it in the building.

The tenant's side

Tenants in New York have a right to a habitable apartment. The state's warranty of habitability, in Real Property Law Section 235-b, covers serious pest conditions, and New York courts have found bed bug infestations can breach it. Tenants can complain to 311, HPD can inspect and issue violations, and in some cases tenants take landlords to Housing Court to get repairs ordered.

Tenants also have responsibilities. They usually need to allow access for inspection and treatment, and to prepare their units when asked. A lot of bed bug disputes start with communication breaking down on one side or the other.

Why your inspection records matter

All of this runs on information. What you disclose, what you report, and how you respond to complaints depends on knowing where bed bugs actually are.

If your inspections rely on an unverified dog, your records are only as good as that dog's guesses. A false alert can put a unit on your disclosure history that never had a problem. A miss can leave you reporting a clean building that wasn't.

Using a certified team doesn't make your records perfect, but it gives you a defensible basis for them. Note the team number, the date, and what was confirmed, and keep it with your bed bug files. When disclosure time comes, you'll be working from facts.
