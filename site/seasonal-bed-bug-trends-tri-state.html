Seasonal Bed Bug Trends in the Tri-State Area

Local insight2 min read

How bed bug activity shifts through the year in New York, New Jersey and Connecticut, and when property managers should schedule K9 inspections.

People tend to think of bed bugs as a summer problem. That's half right. Bed bugs live indoors, at the temperature we keep our homes, so they don't hibernate the way mosquitoes disappear in October. What changes with the seasons isn't the bugs so much as us, and how much we move around.

Summer: travel and turnover

From June through August, the region is in motion. People fly out of Newark and JFK, spend weekends at the Shore or upstate, check into hotels and rentals, and come home with luggage that's been on a lot of floors. Warm buildings also speed up bed bug development, so a small introduction can grow faster.

Summer is also peak moving season in New York City, when a big share of leases turn over. Every move means furniture on sidewalks, boxes in hallways, and belongings packed and unpacked in close quarters.

Late summer into fall: move-in season

August and September bring the student wave, from the big Manhattan campuses to schools in Hoboken, New Haven and Long Island. Dorms and off-campus apartments fill all at once. Small infestations brought in during move-in can take weeks to become noticeable, so problems often surface well into the semester.

Winter: the quiet spread

Cold months can feel calm, but heated buildings stay perfectly comfortable for bed bugs. In New York, where steam heat can push apartments well past 75 degrees in January, activity doesn't stop. Holiday travel adds another round of introductions, and people spending more time indoors means more time in bed, which is when bed bugs feed.

What winter does is slow down detection. Fewer people moving means fewer people noticing. Infestations that started in summer can quietly spread through a building until spring.

What it means for inspections

If you manage property in the tri-state area, a few points on the calendar are worth planning around. Screen vacant units before summer leasing. Screen student and short-term housing before and after the August rush. Do a pass on high-risk buildings in late fall, before the holidays, and again in early spring to catch anything that spread over winter.

And respond to every report quickly, whatever the month. The season changes the odds. It doesn't change the bugs.

Whatever schedule you set, use a certified team. A dog that can't reliably find a light infestation won't help you catch anything early, and early is the whole point.
