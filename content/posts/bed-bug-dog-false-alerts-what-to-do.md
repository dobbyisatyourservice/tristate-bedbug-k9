Every detection dog will eventually alert on something that turns out not to be bed bugs. That isn't a scandal. Dogs are animals, not lab instruments. The question is how often it happens, why, and what you do when it does.

## Why false alerts happen

Some false alerts come from the environment. Old bed bug odor can linger in a building long after an infestation's been treated, especially in cracks and furniture. In New York, where a building might have had bed bugs years ago and nobody wrote it down, that residue is common. A dog that's doing its job might still react to it.

Some come from the dog. Dogs that haven't been trained carefully against distractors can learn to alert on things that smell vaguely similar, or on places that look like where bed bugs usually are.

And some come from the handler. Dogs watch their handlers closely. A handler who expects to find bed bugs in a certain unit can, without meaning to, slow down, linger or cue the dog. The dog obliges.

## How certification helps

A blind field test is designed to catch exactly these problems. In a Tri-State K9 evaluation, the handler doesn't know where the hides are, so they can't cue the dog. The test includes distractors, so a dog that alerts on the wrong odor fails. And it allows no more than one false response across the whole search.

It doesn't make false alerts impossible. It does mean the team has shown, recently and under supervision, that false alerts aren't a habit.

## What to do with an alert you can't confirm

If the dog alerts and a visual inspection finds nothing, don't panic and don't ignore it. A few reasonable options, depending on the building's history and the stakes. Re-inspect in a couple of weeks, when a light infestation would be easier to see. Install monitors near the alert location. Ask the handler how strong and specific the alert was.

What you probably shouldn't do is treat a whole line of apartments based on a single unconfirmed alert, unless there's other evidence. That's expensive, disruptive, and in a rent-stabilized building, very hard to recover.

## Write it down either way

Record the alert, what was checked and what you decided. If the unit turns out to be clean, you have a record showing you followed up. If it turns out to be active later, you have a record showing you took the alert seriously. Either way, you're covered better than if you'd done nothing.
