Every December, under Local Law 69 of 2017, owners of multiple dwellings in New York City have to file their bed bug history with HPD, and make that information available to tenants. For a lot of property managers, it's a scramble. Somebody goes digging through emails and invoices, trying to remember which units had bed bugs eleven months ago.

It doesn't have to be like that. This is a general guide, not legal advice. Check HPD's current requirements, because filing details and forms can change.

## What you're reporting

According to HPD, the filing is due between December 1 and December 31 and covers November 1 through October 31. It asks for the total number of units, how many had an infestation, how many were treated, and how many had bed bugs again after treatment. Owners then give tenants a copy at each new lease or renewal, or post it in the building within 60 days. The same history feeds into what you disclose to new tenants before they sign a lease.

That means you need to know, for each building, which units had confirmed bed bugs, when, what was done and whether it came back.

## Keep a running log

The simplest fix is a log for each building, updated whenever something happens. A spreadsheet works. Record the date of each report, the unit, inspection results, whether bed bugs were confirmed, treatment dates and follow-up results.

Attach the source documents. The K9 inspection report with the team's Tri-State K9 number. The confirmation notes. The treatment invoice. If anyone ever questions a number in your filing, you can show where it came from.

## Separate alerts from confirmed cases

This matters. A K9 alert is an indication. A confirmed infestation is a fact. Your log should show both, but your reporting should be based on what was actually confirmed. If an unreliable dog has been alerting all over your building, your records can end up showing infestations that never existed, or missing ones that did.

That's one more reason to use certified teams. A dog that passed a blind field test recently gives you a much more trustworthy starting point for your records.

## Do a mid-year check

Don't wait until December. Look at your logs in June. Are there units with alerts that were never confirmed or cleared? Treatments without follow-ups? Fix the gaps while people still remember what happened.

## Make it someone's job

The buildings that file smoothly usually have one person responsible for the bed bug log. Not the super, who's busy fixing boilers. Someone at the management office who updates it every time a report comes in. Come December, the filing takes an afternoon instead of a week.
