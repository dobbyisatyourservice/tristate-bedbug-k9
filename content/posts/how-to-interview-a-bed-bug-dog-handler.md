Hiring a K9 team is a bit like hiring a contractor. The best ones are happy to answer questions. The ones you should worry about get defensive, change the subject, or tell you the dog is "never wrong."

Here are the questions we'd ask, and what good answers sound like.

## "What's your team number?"

Start here. A certified handler has a Tri-State K9 team number and will give it to you without fuss. Look it up while you're on the phone. Check that the handler's name and the dog's name match, and that the credential hasn't expired. If the company is sending a different dog than the one listed, ask why.

## "What is the dog trained on?"

You want to hear "bed bugs, only bed bugs." If the dog is also trained on termites or anything else, ask how the handler tells one alert from the other. There isn't a great answer to that, which is why we only certify bed-bug-only dogs.

## "How do you confirm an alert?"

This is the question that separates professionals from salesmen. A good handler will tell you an alert is a strong indication, not a final verdict, and will describe how they confirm it, usually by visual inspection of the spot the dog indicated, or by recommending that your pest control operator does.

Be wary of anyone who treats every alert as proof and goes straight to "you need to treat the whole floor."

## "What happens if the dog alerts somewhere you can't confirm?"

Honest handlers will admit this happens. Ask what they recommend. Re-inspection in a couple of weeks, monitoring devices, a second look. A handler who's comfortable talking about uncertainty is usually one you can trust with the rest of the job.

## "How many units can the dog work in a day?"

Dogs get tired. Their noses get tired. A handler who promises to run a dog through sixty apartments without breaks in an August walk-up in the Bronx is overselling. Good teams pace the work and rest the dog.

## "Who do you work for?"

Some handlers are independent. Some work for pest control companies that also sell treatment. Neither is automatically a problem, but it's worth knowing whether the person finding bed bugs also profits from treating them.

## Write it down

Keep notes from the conversation with the inspection report. If questions come up later from tenants, a board or HPD, you'll be glad you did.
