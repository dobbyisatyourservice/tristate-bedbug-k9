There's no single right answer, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your building is guessing. How often you should bring in a bed bug dog depends on how many people move through the property, how old it is, and what its history looks like. Here's how we'd think about it.

## Start with the history

A building that's had bed bugs in the past couple of years needs more attention than one that hasn't. Old infestations leave behind hiding places and sometimes survivors. If you've treated units recently, a follow-up screen of those lines a few weeks after treatment, and again a few months later, is a sensible baseline.

## Then look at turnover

Turnover is the biggest driver of risk. Every move-in is a chance for bed bugs to arrive.

Hotels turn over constantly, so many screen rooms on a rotating schedule, so that every room gets checked over a set period, plus immediate checks after any guest report. Student housing tends to work around the academic calendar, before move-in, after breaks and before summer programs. Conventional rentals usually do best with a screen at every vacancy, plus periodic checks of common areas and high-risk lines.

## Consider the building itself

Older buildings with shared walls, original plaster and lots of pipe penetrations let bed bugs move more easily between units. A prewar walk-up in Astoria or a big elevator building in the Bronx might justify more frequent screening than a newer building with sealed construction. Buildings with shared laundry rooms, storage areas or package rooms also give bed bugs places to hitch a ride.

## Don't forget the people

Supportive housing, senior buildings, and buildings where many residents work in hotels, healthcare or home care can see more introductions, simply because residents spend time in high-turnover places. That isn't a reason to single anyone out. It's a reason to make screening routine and stigma-free.

## Respond to every report

Whatever schedule you set, it doesn't replace responding quickly when a tenant reports something. Inspect the unit and its neighbors within days, not weeks.

## Use certified teams every time

A routine screening program only works if the dog is accurate. Regularly sending in a team that misses light infestations just gives you a false sense of security on a schedule. Verify every team in the Tri-State K9 registry before they start, and keep the team number on each report.
