Property managers sometimes ask us whether they should use a dog or just have their pest control company inspect. It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that the two methods are good at different things. Most buildings get the best results using both.

## What visual inspection does well

A trained technician with a flashlight can find bed bugs, eggs, shed skins and stains, and when they find them, there's no doubt. That's the big advantage. A visual find is confirmation. It's also what most treatment decisions should ultimately rest on.

The downside is time. A thorough visual inspection of a single apartment can take a long while, especially in a cluttered unit. Mattresses come off, furniture gets pulled out, outlet covers come off. In a light infestation, with only a few bugs hiding deep in a wall void, even a careful technician can miss them.

## What a dog does well

A detection dog covers ground fast. A certified team can screen a unit in a few minutes and a line of apartments in an afternoon. Dogs smell bed bugs in places people can't see, behind baseboards, inside furniture, in wall voids. That makes them especially good at finding light or early infestations, and at clearing units quickly when you need to know where a problem has spread.

The downside is that an alert is an indication, not proof. Even a good dog occasionally alerts on old odor or gets it wrong, and an untested dog can get it wrong often.

## Why they work best together

The strongest approach in a New York building usually looks like this. Use a certified dog to screen widely and quickly, then use visual inspection to confirm alerts before treating. The dog tells you where to look. The technician tells you what's there.

That combination saves time, reduces unnecessary treatments, and catches problems early. It also gives you a clean paper trail: a K9 report from a verified team, followed by confirmation notes.

## The weak link is the untested dog

All of this depends on the dog being accurate. If a dog misses infestations, you're only visually inspecting the units it flagged, and missing the rest. If it alerts too freely, you're paying technicians to chase ghosts.

That's why certification matters. A team listed in the Tri-State K9 registry has passed a blind field test with live bed bugs and distractors in the past year. Look them up before they start.
