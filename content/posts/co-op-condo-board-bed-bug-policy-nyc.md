Bed bugs are awkward in a co-op. In a rental building, the landlord calls the shots. In a co-op or condo, the board has to get shareholders or unit owners to cooperate, and some of them would rather not admit there's a problem in their apartment at all. Somebody always thinks it's the neighbors.

That's why boards that handle bed bugs well have a written policy before they need one.

## Make reporting easy and safe

The worst outcome is a shareholder who hides an infestation because they're embarrassed or worried about cost. By the time it surfaces, three apartments are involved. A good policy makes it clear that reports go to the managing agent, are handled discreetly, and won't be met with blame.

## Spell out access for inspections

House rules or the proprietary lease usually give the board some right of access for repairs and building-wide issues. Your policy should explain how that applies to bed bug inspections, including adjacent units. When an apartment reports bed bugs, the units next to it, above and below need to be checked too. Owners should know that's coming and why.

This is where K9 screening is especially useful in co-ops. A certified team can check several apartments quickly and with less intrusion than a full visual inspection, which helps when owners are reluctant.

## Decide who pays

Cost disputes cause more fights than the bugs do. Some boards cover inspection building-wide and split treatment costs by unit. Others charge the originating unit. There's no single right answer, but there should be a written one, reviewed by the building's counsel.

## Require certified inspectors

Put it in the policy and in the managing agent's vendor contracts: any detection dog used in the building must be listed in the Tri-State K9 registry with a current credential, and the team number goes on every report. It costs the board nothing, and it heads off the "how do we know the dog was right?" argument that otherwise follows every alert in a shareholder's apartment.

## Keep good records

Track every report, inspection and treatment. It's useful for the board, and in New York, owners of multiple dwellings have bed bug reporting obligations to the city. The managing agent should know exactly what's required and when.

## Communicate without alarming

When there's an active case, shareholders in affected lines need to know what's happening and what's expected of them. Keep it factual and brief. Panic spreads faster than bed bugs do.
