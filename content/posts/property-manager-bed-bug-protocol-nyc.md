The buildings that handle bed bugs well aren't the ones that never get them. Every building in New York gets them eventually. The good ones just have a plan before the first call comes in, and they follow it every time.

Here's a protocol that works for most multifamily buildings in the city. Adjust it to your portfolio.

## Day one: take every report seriously

When a tenant reports bites, bugs or stains, log it the same day. Get the unit, the date, what they saw and a photo if they have one. Don't argue with the tenant about whether it's "really" bed bugs. That's what the inspection is for. Getting defensive at this stage is how small problems turn into HPD complaints.

## Within a few days: inspect the unit and its neighbors

Schedule an inspection of the reported unit plus the apartments that share walls, ceilings and floors with it. That usually means the units on either side and directly above and below. In older buildings with shared risers, consider the whole line.

This is where a certified K9 team saves time. A dog can screen a cluster of apartments in an afternoon, and point your pest control operator to the units that need a closer look.

## Confirm before you treat

Treat K9 alerts as strong leads, then confirm them visually where you can. Treating unconfirmed units costs money and goodwill. Skipping a real one costs more.

## Treat, then follow up

Coordinate prep instructions with tenants clearly and in writing, in the languages your tenants actually speak. Schedule follow-up inspections a few weeks after treatment. A single treatment rarely ends the story.

## Keep records like someone will ask for them

Because someone will. Keep the original report, the inspection reports with the team's Tri-State K9 number, confirmation notes, treatment invoices and follow-up results. New York City requires bed bug history disclosure to new tenants and annual reporting to HPD, and your records are what those filings are built on.

## Build in prevention

Screen vacant units before re-renting. Schedule periodic screening in buildings with a history. Train your supers to recognize signs during routine repairs. Put certification requirements into your pest control contracts so you're not relying on whoever the vendor happens to send.

None of this is complicated. The hard part is doing it consistently, every time, at three in the afternoon on a Friday when the call comes in. Write it down, share it with your staff, and stick to it.
